Executive Summary

This report uses Jakob Nielsen’s ten usability heuristics for interaction design. Nielsen’s heuristics are a well-established method of evaluating usability and have remained unchanged since 1994. The heuristics are as follows:

  1. Visibility of System Status
  2. Match Between the System and the Real World
  3. User Control and Freedom
  4. Consistency and Standards
  5. Error Prevention
  6. Recognition Rather than Recall
  7. Flexibility and Efficiency of Use
  8. Aesthetic and Minimalist Design
  9. Helping Users Recognize, Diagnose, and Recover from Errors
  10. Help and Documentation.

We assessed the criteria above in accordance with Nielsen’s severity rating scale, which ranges from 0 to 4: 0 — the absence of a usability problem; 4 — a usability issue that is imperative to address. The online travel marketplace, Airbnb, was the subject of the assessment. Each team member adopted a user persona before exploring the website's core functionalities. These functionalities included sifting through copious search results; finding and securing a home or event for a multi-day trip; and conversely, creating an account to organize an experience as a host. Our audit of the site aimed to identify varied usability flaws that violated Nielsen’s ten heuristics, and we uncovered twelve violations. Once identified, we created an “ease of fixing” scale ranging from 1 to 3, with 3 signifying violations that demanded the most effortful solution. In order of severity, the five most pressing usability issues with an ease of fixing score of 2 or lower were:

  1. Missing back navigation from listing pages.
  2. Missing sort function for search results
  3. Unable to save filters from recent search queries
  4. Lack of centralized support documentation.
  5. No reviews for the host, just individual listings

The usability violations not mentioned above fall under one of the following categories:

  • The violation has an ease of fixing score of 3, demanding a larger redesign.
  • The violation has a severity score of 2 or lower; it’s low priority.

 

Introduction

The report details the heuristic evaluation process and findings for Airbnb’s desktop site, from the guest and the host perspectives. It begins by summarizing what Airbnb is. It then explains our heuristic evaluation methodology, namely, our goals for this analysis and the criteria we used to assess the platform. Using the evaluation criteria, we identified major usability issues sorted by severity and impact on the user experience. We delve into additional detail in the following section, providing context and our recommended solutions. The report concludes with a summary of our findings and the resources used for the heuristic review.

Product Information

Airbnb is an online marketplace for travel & event information as well as booking services. Airbnb offers lodging, home-stay, and tourism services through its websites and mobile applications. Airbnb recently expanded its offerings to include Events and Experiences. Through its affordability and convenience, Airbnb has significantly impacted industries from hospitality to tourism to real estate. The hospitality industry was forced to offer more competitive pricing. Tourism benefited from Airbnb’s design, encouraging visitors to explore hotspots outside of central, tourist-heavy neighborhoods. The business has put a strain on local rental markets, driving up market prices for investors and residents. Alongside services like Uber, Lyft, and TaskRabbit, Airbnb has been a pivotal leader in the sharing economy that’s grown significantly over the past decade.

Methodology

Goals

The goal of this heuristic evaluation is to identify the strengths and weaknesses of the Airbnb website. Our aim is to uncover the design issues that can hinder efficient navigation, task completion, and user satisfaction, as well as to highlight the effective interface features that enhance usability. We will use our findings to inform potential recommendations that can improve the clarity, consistency, and overall user experience of Airbnb.

Heuristics Used

This evaluation was conducted using Jakob Nielsen’s 10 Usability Heuristics for User Interface Design (Nielsen Norman Group, 1994). These heuristics proved a framework for assessing user interfaces and identifying common usability problems related to feedback, consistency, efficiency, and error prevention. Each heuristic served as a lens through which we identified potential issues and classified them according to the principles they violated.

Heuristic

System should still have documentation to guide users in completing tasks efficiently

Evaluation Process and Criteria

Three evaluators independently explored the Airbnb website through both guest and host perspectives. Each evaluator interacted with the key user flows, like searching and filtering, viewing home, experience, and service listings, and completing host onboarding steps. While navigating through the interface, we noted good design features and identified potential usability barriers and inconsistencies. After individual documentation, the team shared our notes and discussed overlapping or related issues to consolidate our findings into distinct problem clusters to avoid redundancy.

Prioritization of Problems

Each issue was rated according to severity and ease of fixing. We used Nielsen’s standard severity ranking scale and created our own ease of fixing rating to estimate implementation difficulty. These ratings helped us prioritize which problems are the most critical to user experience and most feasible to address to improve the website.

Nielsen’s Severity Ranking Scale

        0 - a positive finding / not a usability problem

        1 - cosmetic problem, minor issue, fix if time allows

2 - minor usability problem, fixing is low priority

3 - major usability problem, fixing is high priority

4 - catastrophic, imperative to fix before product can be released

Ease of Fixing Scale

        1 - easy fix, minor text or layout change

2 - moderate fix, requires design/interface adjustment

3 - hard fix, requires functional or structural redesign

Summary of Findings

Our heuristic evaluation of Airbnb’s website identified several recurring usability issues primarily related to navigation, information organization, and cognitive load. While users appreciated features like transparent pricing and clear progress indicators during host onboarding, major pain points emerged during the browsing and booking experience. Users struggled with the lack of a back button after viewing listings, missing sorting options, and inability to save filters during searches. The interface often overwhelmed users with visually similar listings and scattered information, making it difficult to compare options or locate support. These problems collectively contribute to a sense of disorientation and inefficiency when exploring listings. Problem areas

1. No navigation from listing pages

Heuristic: User Control and Freedom        Severity: 4                Ease of Fixing: 2

Issue: During the evaluation, evaluators observed that once a listing is clicked on, there is no clear or consistent way to return to previous search results. This violates the User Control and Freedom heuristic which emphasizes that users should be able to easily undo or exit actions. The absence of a “back” or “return to results” function forces users to manually re-enter their search criteria, filters, and location. This disrupts the task time and leads to frustration, especially when users are looking at and comparing multiple listings. We classified the issue as a major usability problem (Severity: 3) since the issue directly interrupts core user tasks, though recovery is possible through manual re-navigation, requiring extra cognitive effort.

Evidence: Figure 1 is a screenshot of an individual Airbnb listing which demonstrates the absence of a visible navigation element that would allow users to return to their previous search results. Neither a back button nor a breadcrumb trail is present at the top of the interface, leaving users without a clear way to go back to their search. As a result, users must manually re-enter their search parameters or use the browser’s back function, which may not restore prior filters or scroll position.

Figure 1: Individual listing without “back” navigation control

Recommendation: Airbnb should include a persistent, clearly labeled “Back to Results” navigation element on each listing page. This feature should restore users to their previous search view while retaining prior filters, sorting options, and scroll positions. Implementing this would reduce unnecessary repetition of actions, maintain user orientation, and improve users’ sense of control. Additionally, consistent navigation behaviour across the website would increase predictability and trust in the interface. Adding a back button would be a moderate fix (Ease of fix: 2) since slight interface adjustment would be required.

2. No sort function for search results

Heuristic: Flexibility and Efficiency of Use                  Serverity: 3                  Ease of Fixing: 2

Issue: During navigation, evaluators noted that there was no sort function for Airbnb’s desktop search results. For tourist-heavy destinations, Airbnb can output over 1,000 properties to sift through. There’s an easily accessible filter function to narrow down the results. Nevertheless, even with significant constraints, the results are still overwhelming. This violates the Flexibility and Efficiency of Use Heuristic—shortcuts speed up the interaction for expert users, so the design can cater to both inexperienced and experienced users. The absence of a common navigation element on an e-commerce site forces users to look through listings manually. When a user is in the early stages of deciding on a home-stay, the absence of a sort tool can make a daunting task feel nearly unapproachable. We assigned a severity rating of 3, as this severely impedes the user’s use of one of the website’s core offerings.

Evidence: Figure 2 displays the search results screen for a fairly generic query on Airbnb. In the upper right-hand corner, where one would typically find “Sort,” only “Filters” is present. Both filter and sort functions offer a way to organize large amounts of information, simplifying the decision-making process to fit the specific needs of the user. When not placed adjacent to “Filters”, e-commerce sites have been known to embed their sort function within a filter function, creating a “Sort and Filters” button. To be thorough, we opened the filters modal further to verify that the sort was non-existent, not hidden. There was nothing to be found.

Figure 2: Search results missing a sort feature.

Recommendation: Airbnb should include a persistent sort function that rests either within the filters function or as a standalone feature next to filters. The sort function should default to some recommended order upon landing on the search results page. Users should have the option to sort by price, popularity, user ratings, and when items were added or created, in both ascending and descending order. Adding a sort function shouldn’t be a taxing fix, given that engineers sort by criteria that are already available. More niche sort criteria may be more involved.

3. Overwhelming visual clutter and similar listings

Heuristic: User Control and Freedom        Severity: 3                Ease of Fixing: 3

Issue: Evaluators found that Airbnb’s search results display numerous listings that used similar images, layout styles, and text descriptions. This lack of visual differentiation violates the Aesthetic and Minimalist Design Heuristic, as users struggle to visually parse or compare options effectively. The repetition of similar thumbnails and text contributes to visual noise and decision fatigue, which increases cognitive load during browsing. The issue also touches on the Recognition Rather than Recall heuristic, as users may have difficulty remembering which listings they have already viewed. We classified this issue as a major usability problem (Severity: 3) due to its impact on search efficiency and decision-making.

Evidence: The screenshot (Figure 3) of Airbnb’s search results demonstrates how listings appear visually repetitive and lack clear differentiation. Many listings use similar color palettes, room compositions, and camera angles, creating a monotonous array of thumbnails. Users have to click into each listing to find unique features and amenities, which increases cognitive load and makes it difficult for users to compare options efficiently.

Figure 3: Search results showing repetitive visual composition and minimal differentiation between listings

Recommendation: To reduce visual clutter and cognitive load, Airbnb should adopt a more distinct visual hierarchy within the search results. This can be achieved by creating criteria for hosts to follow when choosing cover images. Search results could also include a line emphasizing differentiating features like unique amenities (ie pool, kitchen, AC, etc). Adding meaningful distinctions would help users compare listings more effectively and search with less fatigue. Addressing this issue would be difficult (Ease of fix: 3), since it involves multiple levels of design refinements, like layout restructuring, data reorganization, and backend changes to enable the system to prompt hosts with image selection guidelines, provide feedback on image distinctiveness, and be able to pick out unique listing features.

4. Unstructured listings

Heuristic: Consistency and standards        Severity: 2                Ease of Fixing: 2

Issue: Evaluators found the lack of a consistent structure in listing photo galleries confusing. Because hosts control both the selection and order of images, users struggle to locate key visual information—such as overall room layout or the condition/atmosphere of the kitchen and bathroom. This runs counter to the “Consistency and standards” heuristic, which emphasizes predictable structures to support efficient navigation across a service. We classified this as a minor usability problem (Severity: 2) since it doesn’t interrupt user’s core tasks but it still puts burdens on some users with specific purposes.

Evidence:

As shown in Figure 4, the initial gallery view varies widely across listings. Some open with interior shots (e.g., bedroom), while others begin with exterior images. This inconsistency forces users to click through multiple photo views to find comparable information.

Figure 4: Pictures used of four different listings’ first landing page.

Recommendation: To make comparison easier and reduce unnecessary navigation, Airbnb should publish guidelines for the first five photos in every listing (e.g., wide shot of the main room, layout/floor plan if available, kitchen, bathroom, and one exterior/context image). This would let users compare options without opening the full gallery. At the same time—and in light of Finding 3 (“Overwhelming visual clutter and similar listings”)—the very first cover image should remain flexible so hosts can highlight a distinctive feature that differentiates their listing. It would be a moderate fix (Ease of fix: 2) since all Airbnb needs to do is to decide the structure and announce it, but it requires hosts to change the structure of photos of their listings, which takes time for transition.

5. Unable to save filters

Heuristic: Consistency and Standards                  Serverity: 2                 Ease of Fixing: 2

Issue: Evaluators found that Airbnb doesn’t provide a method of saving filters used in previous searches. Airbnb has many listings, and filters are integral to narrowing them down to something manageable and scannable. This defies the Lack of Consistency and Standard Heuristic. Users will change the search criteria if the initial searches don’t output results that fit their needs. Nevertheless, the constraints that one needs to abide by, especially regarding accessibility, will remain constant. Having to re-select the filters for each query is tedious and grows repetitive after each search, but it doesn’t make navigation impossible.

Evidence: Figure 5 on the left displays the initial search. For this search, evaluators selected three accessibility options from the list available. Evaluators then navigated to the home page and performed another search with the same criteria. Figure 5 displays the nav bar with 3 filters applied, indicated by a badge in the bottom right corner. Figure 6 displays the nav bar after the second search. Note the lack of a badge in Figure 5. Figure 7 also displays the pop-up modal that appears when the button is clicked.

Figure 5: The first search with the initial search criteria and accessibility features applied.

Figure 6: The second search with the same search criteria. Note that the filters have reset.

Figure 7: Airbnb’s Filters pop-up modal

Recommendation: Saving any filter preferences would add unnecessary visual clutter to the interface and an extraneous memory cost. Save a user’s most recent filter selections to prevent the repetitive labor of having to re-enter them with each query. The ease of fixing score is 2, requiring a slight interface adjustment for the filters pop-up modal. Allowing users to save filters to their profiles and input filter presets depending on their needs would be more difficult to implement, but would be a more robust solution

6. No map context

Heuristic: Match between systems and real world        Severity: 2                Ease of Fixing: 2

Issue: Proximity to public transit and tourist attractions is a key factor when selecting accommodation, yet evaluators found Airbnb’s map makes these points of interest hard to find. Users often approximate a listing’s location on Airbnb and then switch to an external map (e.g., Google Maps) to gauge distances to likely destinations. This conflicts with the “Match Between System and the Real World” heuristic, which states that interfaces should mirror familiar real-world structures and conventions so users don’t have to decipher terminology or controls. In this case, the Airbnb map should work more like mainstream mapping apps―supporting straightforward search and easy identification of popular destinations―so users can estimate distances without leaving the site. We consider this a minor usability issue (Severity 2) because it doesn’t block core tasks, though it can burden users pursuing specific purposes.

Evidence: As shown in Figure 8. Airbnb’s map does not support searching for transit stops or tourist attractions. When users zoom in to locate a potential destination, they can lose track of the listing they were viewing. Without searchable or persistent references to popular destinations, it’s difficult to identify areas that are conveniently close to multiple points of interest.

 

Figure 8: Map indicating the listing’s location (left: wide view; right: close-up view)

Recommendation: To streamline comparison across listings, Airbnb should add a built-in search for transit stops and tourist attractions, as in other popular mapping apps. While showing precise travel times could compromise listing privacy and should be avoided, providing a clear visual sense of approximate distance would help users identify suitable areas. Additionally. A pin/flag feature would allow users to mark key destinations and quickly see which neighborhoods―or which candidate listings―are convenient to most of their planned stops, making map-based comparison easier. Addressing this issue requires a moderate fix (Ease of fix: 2) since the Airbnb map is already retrieving information from Google Map and they only need to add a search function. However, pin/flag feature requires several layers of design considerations, which may take time.

7. Support hard to find

Heuristic: Help and documentation / visibility of system status Severity: 2 Ease of Fixing: 2

Issue: Evaluators struggled to locate Airbnb support when they encountered host-related issues before check-in. Because access to support is buried at the bottom of the interface, users can feel anxious, worrying they might not be able to get assistance. This conflicts with the “Help and Documentation” heuristic, which emphasizes that help should be easy to find and use when needed. We classified this as a minor usability issue (Severity 2): it becomes an issue only when the user encounters a serious issue that they can’t handle directly with the host, but it causes an issue when it happens.

Evidence: The support entry appears only after a long block of trip details. During initial scans, evaluators overlooked it because it is visually undifferentiated and formatted similarly to other booking information.  

Recommendation: Airbnb should make support more visible by adding a persistent, fixed “Support” bar at the bottom of the screen so users can access help without scrolling. The bar should be visually distinct and present across booking and messaging flows, providing clear entry points to self-help and live assistance. This would reduce anxiety and shorten time-to-help, especially when issues arise before check-in. It would be a moderate fix (Ease of fix: 2), considering the importance of information hierarchy in the booking information page and time required to test out where to locate the help button.

8. Cannot sort/navigate through host profile/reviews

Heuristic: Flexibility and efficiency of use         Severity: 2                Ease of Fixing: 2

Issue: Host reliability is critical on peer-to-peer platforms like Airbnb. Some guests want to read host-level, cross-listing reviews to assess a host’s sense of responsibility. However, Airbnb does not provide a way to filter or search reviews that specifically discuss the host. This conflicts with the “Flexibility and efficiency of use” heuristic, which calls for tailored paths and shortcuts that help users quickly achieve their goals. We rate this a minor usability issue (Severity 2): it doesn’t impede core tasks, but it adds friction for users with specific goals.

Evidence: Guests can view reviews from all of a host’s listings, but some of these reviews talk only about the specific property and say little or nothing about the host. In Figure 10, for example, only one of the three reviews mentions the host; the other two describe the individual stays.

Figure 10: Only one of three reviews on the host’s profile addresses the host; the other two focus on the specific listings.

Figure 11: The Reviews section lacks filters or sorting to surface host-specific feedback.

Recommendation: To support guests who care about a host’s reliability and personality, Airbnb should add filters and search for host-specific feedback (e.g., “mentions host,” “communication,” “responsiveness”).. Airbnb already lets hosts review guests to vet the people they accept; by the same logic, guests should be able to easily vet the person responsible for their stay. Providing host-level review controls would deliver a more customized and efficient experience. It would be a moderate fix (Ease of Fix: 2) since it requires separating a review specifically on the host, which is already asked in a separate category of the review guest's answer, and adding a search function in the host review.

9. Ambiguous icons and “guest favorite” label overuse

Heuristic: Match between System and Real World                Severity: 1        Ease of Fixing: 1

Issue: Throughout the search results, evaluators noted an overuse of the “Guest Favorite” label and inconsistent presentation of the trophy icon. Some listings displayed both the label and icon, while others featured only the label, without any explanation of what the designation means. This inconsistency violates the Match Between System and the Real World, as users cannot easily infer the label’s criteria. The overuse of these indicators diminishes their perceived value and can confuse users. The lack of an explanatory key further adds to the ambiguity. We categorized the issue as a minor usability problem (Severity: 1) since it contributes to mild confusion but does not prevent task completion.

Evidence: This problem occurs throughout the Airbnb search results. As seen in Figure 12, the search results page shows multiple listings labeled “Guest favorite,” with only some including the trophy icon. At a glance, the “Guest favorite’ without trophy has a higher rating than one of the “Guest favorite” with trophy, which creates ambiguity about the trophies significance or criteria. Additionally, every listing in the screenshot is labelled as “Guest favorite” which dilutes its meaning.

Figure 12: Overuse and inconsistency of labels and icons across listings

Recommendation: Airbnb should establish consistent usage criteria for the “Guest favorite” label and trophy icon. Including a key or tooltip would help users interpret the meaning of the designations accurately. Furthermore, reducing the frequency of the labels and applying it only to listings that meet specified standards would enhance its credibility and restore its intended value. We categorized these recommendations as relatively easy (Ease of fix: 1) since the changes primarily require content and design consistency updates. Brief explanatory text and standardizing icon usage can be implemented with minimal technical changes.

10. No accessibility / kid-friendly tags

Heuristic: Match between System and Real World                Severity: 1        Ease of Fixing: 2

Issue: Airbnb’s ease of access has allowed it to scale significantly. When searching for a place, a user is presented with plenty of options, but this creates a new issue: needing to narrow down the results to something applicable. Evaluators adopted the persona of a major user group when discovering this usability issue: parents. Finding lodging that meets a user's family's specific needs can be challenging; every family has different needs and preferences. Nevertheless, there are universal concerns that every parent takes into account when traveling with young children—baby-proofing, more broadly, child safety. Airbnb’s inclusion of essential accessibility tags, such as step-free access and wide entrances, shows, at minimum, a step toward inclusion. To reflect your diverse consumer base, Airbnb shouldn’t be missing constraints that meet the needs of its various traveler groups. This issue has a severity rank of 1 because it doesn’t severely impede the user’s experience. There are combinations of other filter criteria that can be leveraged to fit the needs of a particular family. Nevertheless, parents traveling with children— deviating from an established schedule—won’t deny having some peace of mind.

Evidence: Figure 13 is a screenshot of the expanded Amenities menu. Options are present, but a crib is the only amenity tailored specifically for children. As a result, users must assume that selecting “crib” will result in a home-stay that’s child-friendly and/or has other amenities tailored to families.

Figure 13: A screenshot of the expanded amenities menu

Recommendation: Airbnb should expand its amenities filter to include options that address the needs of its major user groups. Depending on how extensive the menu is, the options could include a nested drop-down for each specific user group. For instance, within the Amenities menu, there could be an additional nested menu explicitly stating “For parents”. Expanded, that menu would display additional amenity filter options like outlet covers, highchairs, Pack' n Plays, and toys. Ease of fixing score is 2 because a slight interface adjustment would be needed to accommodate the additional filter options.

Discussion and General Recommendations

During the evaluation, broader patterns revealed issues related to navigation efficiency, information overload, and inconsistent interface behaviour. Many usability problems stemmed from gaps in feedback and control. Users frequently lost their place or had to repeat actions like reapplying filters. Visual clutter and repetitive listings further increased cognitive load. To improve overall usability, Airbnb should focus on streamlining navigation flows, reducing and preventing redundant visual elements, and incorporating filters, sorting, and back functions that behave predictably. Strengthening consistency across these core interactions would create a more seamless experience for users.

Conclusion

We evaluated Airbnb’s website―an accommodation-listing and booking platform―using Jakob Nielsen’s ten usability heuristics. Overall, the evaluators identified several features that conflict with heuristics intended to ensure usability for diverse goals, even though some of the same elements can make exploration feel playful. Because Airbnb began as a peer-to-peer marketplace, flexibility (e.g., hosts can write descriptions and arrange photos without a fixed template) supports authenticity and variety.

From a usability perspective, however, this openness can lead to disorientation and inefficiency. We recommend balancing individuality with consistency by introducing light structure, sorting functions, and clearer visual hierarchies: for example, standardized sort option for search results; visual banners to differentiate listings; and a predefined order for the first few photos (while keeping the cover image flexible). Adding more scannable information to results pages would further reduce cognitive load and help users compare options without opening each listing.
